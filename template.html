200 mg on the label. Not 200 mg absorbed.

That 200 mg is what's in the capsule, not a measured absorbed dose. Oxide and bisglycinate can both provide magnesium, but human studies don't establish one absorption rate for either form. This label also doesn't state the split between them. The absorbed amount cannot be calculated from the label, so a big number doesn't settle the question.

What human studies have measured

One commercial oxide preparation showed 4% fractional absorption estimated from urinary excretion in normal volunteers (Firoz & Graber, 2001). In 12 people with ileal resection, diglycinate and oxide measured 23.5% and 22.8% overall, without a significant overall difference (Schuette et al., 1994). A review found dose and magnesium status often matter more than salt. None of these studies tested either finished product here or establishes a minimum absorption advantage for bisglycinate.

About the math and human evidence

Theoretical percentages use anhydrous chemical composition (bisglycinate 14.1%, citrate 16%, oxide 60%). Actual ingredient grades can assay differently. Capsule sizes specify volume; this example converts volume to a powder-weight range using 0.6–1.0 g/mL. It does not include fillers or measure a specific product. Capsule size and density reference.

Walker et al. (2003) found greater urinary magnesium excretion with organic forms than oxide after 60 days in 46 people, while citrate had the highest mean serum values. Schuette et al. (1994) found no significant overall fractional-absorption difference between chelate and oxide in 12 people with ileal resection, though a small impaired-absorption subgroup favoured chelate. These studies do not compare Freshfield's finished product with this Canadian label or predict an individual shopper's absorption.

For formulas combining forms, read each medicinal ingredient; this single-form estimate cannot determine a blend. Always check serving instructions. No absorption or health outcome can be inferred from this calculation.
